Which mode should you use: aperture priority for portraits, shutter priority for sports, or manual mode once you are “serious”? Those shortcuts miss the real decision: **which variable do you most need to control right now?**

**Short answer:** P delegates aperture and shutter selection, A keeps aperture under your control, S keeps shutter speed under your control, and M gives you direct control of both. ISO remains a separate choice. No mode is inherently more professional or produces better photographs.

## Start with the decision, not the letter

| Mode                      | You choose directly                                                | With fixed ISO, the camera adjusts | Useful when your priority is                         |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **P — Program AE**        | Neither aperture nor shutter; program shift may offer alternatives | Aperture and shutter combination   | A quick start with optional intervention             |
| **A — Aperture Priority** | Aperture                                                           | Shutter speed                      | Depth of field and aperture-dependent rendering      |
| **S — Shutter Priority**  | Shutter speed                                                      | Aperture                           | Motion blur or camera-shake control                  |
| **M — Manual Exposure**   | Aperture and shutter speed                                         | Neither                            | Consistent settings or deliberate exposure decisions |

Some cameras label shutter priority **Tv** and aperture priority **Av**. The idea is the same, but button layout and automation are model-specific.

## ISO is a separate automation decision

Manual exposure means direct control of aperture and shutter speed; it does not necessarily mean every setting is manual. Auto ISO can add another automatic variable, depending on the camera and configuration. Likewise, choosing A or S does not force ISO to be automatic.

Before judging a mode, check all three settings: aperture, shutter speed, and ISO. The [exposure-stops lesson](../exposure-stops/) explains why compensating one with another can preserve brightness while changing motion, depth of field, or noise.

## Worked shooting decisions

- **Static architecture in changing light:** choose A and an aperture that suits the scene. Watch the shutter speed the camera proposes; depth of field does not protect against camera shake.
- **A walking subject:** choose S and a shutter time that gives the motion rendering you want. Watch whether the lens can open wide enough. A fast shutter does not guarantee accurate focus.
- **Consistent studio or repeated product frames:** M can keep aperture and shutter fixed as you adjust one light or reflector at a time.
- **An unplanned first frame:** P gets an automatic starting combination. Program shift, when available, can select a different combination with a similar automatic exposure target.

These are example decisions, not universal recommended settings. Inspect the actual proposal and its limits.

## X-T5 example: modes come from control positions

The X-T5 does not use the common single PASM mode dial described for some other cameras. For still photography, the official manual establishes the mode through shutter-speed and aperture controls:

1. **P:** set shutter speed and lens aperture to **A** (auto).
2. **S:** choose a shutter speed and set lens aperture to **A**.
3. **A:** set shutter speed to **A** and choose the aperture.
4. **M:** choose both shutter speed and aperture.

With the XF16–50mm, use its aperture ring. Other lenses and customized controls can change the exact procedure. A red value or another warning means the automatic system cannot reach its target within the available range; changing the compensation number alone does not remove that physical limit.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Manual mode always gives the best exposure.”** It gives responsibility, not automatic correctness. Metering, highlights, and intent still need attention.
- **“Aperture priority controls focus.”** It controls aperture selection; autofocus and depth of field are related but separate decisions.
- **“The camera can always compensate.”** Lens aperture limits, shutter range, ISO range, flash, and scene light can make a requested combination impossible.
- **“Auto ISO means the sensor receives more light.”** It changes the ISO setting and its output mapping; aperture and shutter still determine the optical exposure.

## Practice: observe the delegated variable

Use a safe, static scene with steady light and fixed ISO. Start at exposure compensation zero.

1. In A, select a middle aperture available on your lens. Make one frame near a window and one facing a darker part of the room without changing your aperture.
2. Record the shutter speeds and compare brightness. Explain why the shutter changed.
3. In S, choose a shutter speed appropriate for a stationary subject. Repeat the same change of direction and record the aperture values.
4. Repeat in M with both settings fixed. This time, observe what no longer compensates automatically.

Return any altered controls to a known state afterward. The goal is to predict behavior, not collect a supposedly perfect set of numbers.
