Shutter speed usually means exposure duration: 1/250 s is a shorter time than 1/60 s. It controls how movement is spread across the frame while light is collected.

**Short answer:** choose the time for the motion you want, then solve the remaining exposure trade-offs. There is no single shutter speed that guarantees every moving subject will be sharp.

## Four kinds of “blurry photo”

| Cause                      | Clue                                                        | Useful response                                           |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Subject motion             | Moving areas trail; stationary areas may be sharp           | Shorter time, timing, or intentional panning              |
| Camera shake               | Much of the frame shows a directional smear                 | Support, stabilization, shorter time, steadier technique  |
| Missed focus               | A different plane is sharper, or nothing is sharply focused | Focus mode, point placement, aperture and distance review |
| Rolling-shutter distortion | Straight moving objects skew, or light bands appear         | Appropriate shutter mode and model-specific settings      |

Image stabilization can compensate for some **camera motion**. It does not freeze a running child or a bird's wings.

## Motion is relative to the frame

A person walking across the frame can blur differently from one walking toward you. A subject at the edge of a wide view crosses fewer pixels per second than the same subject in a tight view. Camera panning can keep a moving subject relatively steady while streaking the background, but requires practice.

## Worked example settings

At **1/30 s**, a deliberate pan might render a cyclist sharply against a blurred background. At **1/500 s**, both rider and background may look much more frozen. These are example settings, not assurances; speed, direction, framing, focus, and technique all matter.

Changing 1/125 s to 1/250 s halves exposure time: a one-stop reduction. If you compensate with ISO, the incoming light has not increased; you changed the output side of the chain. If you compensate with aperture, depth of field may change.

## Mechanical versus electronic shutter

Electronic shutters can be quiet and fast, but some sensors read different rows at different times. Motion can lean or deform, and some artificial lighting can produce bands. A fast displayed shutter value does not necessarily mean the whole sensor was sampled simultaneously. Consult your manual for flash, flicker reduction, and readout limitations.

## Common misconceptions

- **The reciprocal-of-focal-length rule guarantees sharp handholding.** It is only a rough historical heuristic, not a guarantee across resolutions, stabilization, and subject motion.
- **More stabilization stops means every subject is sharp.** Stabilization addresses certain camera movements, not focus or subject motion.
- **A blurred background proves a shallow depth of field.** Background streaks from motion can look superficially similar but have a different cause.

## Practice: one moving subject, three times

In a safe location, photograph a predictable moving subject with example times 1/30, 1/125, and 1/500 s. Keep framing and focus method consistent. Compare subject sharpness and motion trails; then try a gentle pan at the slower time. Record the actual result rather than treating the numbers as pass/fail thresholds.
