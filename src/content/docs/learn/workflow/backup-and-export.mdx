You selected three photographs. What should you protect, and what should you send?

<strong>Short answer:</strong> Keep the complete original shoot, check a backup
that can survive loss of your working storage, and export selected copies for
one stated use. Reopen the actual export and inspect both the image and its
identifying information locally. Selecting, backing up, and exporting answer
different questions.

<strong>Start here:</strong> Take the small shoot from [Photo Selection and
Review](../../field-practice/photo-review/), or another shoot. Keep its source
intact. Choose one photograph for a local web-preview export; this exercise
needs no public post. Open [Keep originals, export a
copy](../../../practice/backup-and-export/) for the three actions and your own
completion record.

## Keep the whole shoot while deciding what to show

The C4 shortlist records what serves your present purpose. It does not replace the complete source set. During this exercise retain selected and unselected frames, including both RAW and JPEG if you captured both. If you captured only JPEG, those camera files are your originals; RAW is not a requirement.

An original is the captured file you preserve. An exported derivative is an output prepared from it, potentially with changed rendering, crop, dimensions, compression, or metadata. A small JPEG cannot retain all RAW capture data. “Keep originals” also does not mean “never make editorial choices”: you can change flags and albums without erasing the source evidence. Review [RAW, JPEG, HEIF, and EXIF](../../image-quality/raw-jpeg-exif/) when the roles are unclear.

## Copy first, then check recovery

Use trusted local software to **copy rather than move** the complete small shoot. Keep the card or other source intact. Check which formats and files reached the working destination; visible thumbnails alone are not a complete transfer check.

Make a backup on separate physical storage. Another folder or partition on the same drive shares that drive’s failure risk. Check coverage and recover a representative sample from the backup into a separate folder, without replacing your working files. Open recovered RAW and JPEG samples when both are relevant, using compatible software. Compare expected file counts and sizes as useful checks, not proof that every byte is correct.

A sample recovery tests only the sampled files and that recovery path. It cannot certify every file, future readability, or protection against every incident. If separate storage is unavailable, plan the step and leave its task checkbox unmarked until you can perform it.

<details>
<summary>What about synchronization, catalogs, and 3-2-1?</summary>

Synchronization keeps locations aligned; deletion may travel with it. Apple documents this behavior for iCloud Photos. A service may also offer version history or recovery, but check what it preserves and for how long before relying on it as backup.

A Lightroom Classic catalog backup contains the catalog, not your photograph files. Preserve actual originals and any editing records your workflow needs. The widely used **3-2-1** strategy calls for three copies, two media types, and one offsite copy. It is a planning aid, not a guarantee: separation, retention, coverage, and recovery checks still matter. An offline copy reduces some shared risks. This local exercise does not require buying a service or connecting an account.

</details>

Do not delete or format a card for this task. Before a later destructive card operation, verify the copies and recovery coverage you need and follow the camera manual. Never interrupt a card write. For the clearly labeled X-T5 example, Fujifilm states that formatting also removes protected pictures; a protection mark is not a backup.

## Export for one destination

Write the purpose on paper or in your own local notes: “ordinary web preview,” “a print lab’s requested file,” or “full-size delivery.” The website stores none of those notes. Export one to three C4 selections into a separate destination so a naming collision cannot silently replace an original.

For an ordinary SDR web preview, **JPEG with sRGB** is one practical example when supported. Choose pixel dimensions and compression for the recipient’s requirements; there is no universal long-edge size or quality percentage. For print or another workflow, follow that destination’s specifications instead.

**Pixel dimensions** say how many pixels the exported image contains. **PPI** relates those pixels to a physical print size. Changing a PPI label alone does not create detail or determine a web image’s pixel dimensions; check whether a resize operation also resamples the image.

## Inspect the exported file itself

Reopen the actual exported file in a local viewer. Check orientation, crop, necessary detail, dimensions, and rendering at the intended viewing size. An editor preview or “export complete” message does not answer those questions.

Use a trusted local metadata tool to inspect location and identifying fields. Check the filename and visible image too: a street sign, face, name badge, or home interior can disclose information even without GPS. Choose what the audience needs, then inspect the result after any metadata change. Removing GPS is not a promise of anonymity; unresolved fields remain unknown.

Adobe’s example export controls can remove GPS for some formats, but that option is unavailable for DNG. Other software and formats behave differently. Preserve your private originals, inspect the delivery copy, and decide separately whether to share. This website neither reads EXIF nor verifies privacy.
