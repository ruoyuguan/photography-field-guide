When you press the shutter button, the camera does not simply “save what the lens sees.” It performs a chain of optical, mechanical, electronic, and computational steps.

**Short answer:** the lens projects light, the sensor samples it, electronics read and convert the signal, and processing produces RAW data or a rendered image.

## The path of a photograph

```text
scene light → lens aperture → shutter → sensor photosites
            → readout and analog-to-digital conversion
            → RAW data → demosaic and image processing → JPEG / HEIF / TIFF
```

The sensor is covered by many light-sensitive locations, often called photosites. In a typical color camera, color-filter information is reconstructed into a color image during demosaicing. A photosite is not automatically identical to a finished image pixel in every processing context.

## What each stage controls

- **Aperture and shutter time** influence the exposure reaching the sensor while the shutter is open.
- **Focus** determines which parts of the projected image are sharply resolved at the sensor plane.
- **Sensor size** sets the recording area, affecting field of view with a given lens and camera position.
- **Readout and conversion** turn the captured signal into digital data.
- **ISO-related processing and output development** influence the rendered signal. ISO does not create more incoming light.

## Example settings, followed through the camera

Consider **F2.8, 1/250 s, ISO 800**. The aperture and 1/250-second exposure act before or during light collection. ISO 800 belongs to the subsequent capture/output chain; its exact implementation varies by camera and mode. A RAW file and a JPEG can come from the same exposure but receive different processing. EXIF records information about the file and settings; it is not a second image or proof of every physical condition.

## Mechanical and electronic shutters

A mechanical shutter uses moving physical elements. Electronic shutter modes read the sensor electronically; some implementations read rows at different times, which can skew fast motion or interact with pulsing lights. Read your model's manual rather than assuming every electronic shutter behaves identically.

## Common misconceptions

- **The sensor sees color exactly like a human eye.** It samples filtered signals; reconstruction and rendering are required.
- **RAW is an unprocessed photograph ready for every use.** It preserves sensor-oriented data that usually requires development and is not a universal finished-image format.
- **ISO makes the sensor collect more photons.** It does not change the light that arrived during the exposure.

## Practice: separate capture from development

Use a stationary subject and example settings that give a usable image. Save RAW plus JPEG if your camera supports it. Keep the same exposure and make one JPEG with a different in-camera picture style, if available. Compare the files: the scene and incoming light did not change, but output processing did.
