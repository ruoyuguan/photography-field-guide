**Short answer:** ask **how the scene reaches your eye**, then separately ask **what records it** and **whether the lens changes**. Those are different questions. A film SLR and a digital SLR share a reflex viewing principle; a fixed-lens camera can still use a sensor-fed electronic finder.

**Try first:** without pointing at the sun or another intense light source, look through your camera finder and read its manual. Is it an optical view or a display? A top hump or an offset eyepiece alone cannot establish the answer. Start from the [camera classification overview](../../../cameras/) if the names are unfamiliar.

## 1 · SLR: one taking lens, two moments

In a typical **single-lens reflex (SLR)**, the taking lens also supplies the normal optical finder view. The diagram groups the focusing screen and finder optics; it does not trace the internal prism reflections.

<CameraOpticalPaths locale="en" kind="slr" state="exposure" />

The mirror moves clear before recording; opening the shutter permits exposure at the recording plane. Mirror motion and shutter timing are distinct actions. This illustrates a conventional moving-mirror design, not every historical reflex variant or every camera mode.

</details>

**SLR does not mean digital.** Canon FX is a film SLR; DSLR adds “digital” to describe the recording system. Changing film to a sensor does not, by itself, define the finder path.

## 2 · TLR: two lenses do different jobs

A **twin-lens reflex (TLR)** uses one lens for viewing and another for taking the photograph. In the Rolleiflex 2.8 FX example, the top focusing screen supports waist-level viewing; alternative finder attachments exist.

<CameraOpticalPaths locale="en" kind="tlr" />

The two viewpoints can produce **parallax**: close subjects do not occupy precisely the same position in both views. Framing compensation is model-dependent. “Waist-level” describes how you look into a finder; it is not a synonym for TLR.

## 3 · Coupled rangefinder: measure separately from taking

In a **coupled rangefinder**, adjusting lens focus is linked to a measuring system. On the Leica M6, you align a split or superimposed image patch. The normal finder view is separate from the taking lens, so its framing also needs parallax compensation.

<CameraOpticalPaths locale="en" kind="rangefinder" />

An offset finder or a rectangular “rangefinder-style” body does not prove that this measuring mechanism exists. A simple direct optical finder may help compose without measuring focus. **An optical viewfinder alone does not make a camera an SLR.**

## 4 · Digital mirrorless: light becomes information, then a display

A modern digital **mirrorless** camera does not use an SLR reflex mirror to form its normal preview. The sensor supplies image information to an electronic finder (EVF), a rear screen, or both.

<CameraOpticalPaths locale="en" kind="mirrorless" />

The dashed path is electronic information, not scene light reflected to your eye. The display emits new light. Preview appearance depends on camera settings and display limits; it is not a guarantee of the final image.

**Mirrorless and interchangeable-lens are separate descriptions.** GFX100RF, for example, has an electronic finder and a fixed, single-focal-length lens. **Fixed lens** means the lens is not user-interchangeable; **prime lens** means its focal length does not zoom. Neither expression means fixed focus. The Chinese market term “微单” (weidan) is common in everyday usage; check the viewing architecture and lens system separately rather than treating that label as an additional optical mechanism.

## Check the distinction before looking at the answer

**A camera has an EVF and a non-interchangeable 35 mm autofocus lens. Must it be a DSLR, or must its focus be fixed?**

<details>
<summary>Reveal the reasoning</summary>

Neither conclusion follows. The finder, lens interchangeability, focal-length range, and focusing method describe different properties. Read each relevant specification separately. GFX100RF is a concrete example of why an EVF and a fixed prime lens do not imply a reflex mirror or fixed focus.

</details>

For your own camera, find three manual entries: **finder type**, **recording medium**, and **lens system**. Explain each in one sentence. You can do this entirely on paper; this page stores no answers and requires no account or photographs.
