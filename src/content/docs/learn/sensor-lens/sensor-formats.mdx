A camera's **sensor format** describes the physical size of the area that captures light. “Full frame” and “APS-C” are format names, not measures of image quality by themselves.

| Format     | Approximate sensor dimensions            | Typical crop factor |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Full frame | 36 × 24 mm                               | 1×                  |
| APS-C      | About 23.5 × 15.7 mm in the X-T5 example | About 1.5×          |

APS-C dimensions vary by manufacturer and sometimes by camera model. Use a specific camera's specifications when exact dimensions matter.

## What a smaller sensor changes

With the same lens and camera position, a smaller sensor records a smaller part of the image circle. The result is a **narrower field of view**, similar to cropping the full-frame image.

At the same f-number and shutter speed, the smaller sensor also collects less total light because its area is smaller. This can affect noise performance and the range of depth-of-field effects you can achieve; it is not a rule that every smaller sensor is noisier in every comparison.

## What it does not change

- **The lens focal length stays the same.** A 50 mm lens is still a 50 mm lens.
- **The f-number does not change.** f/2.8 does not become another physical aperture setting.
- **Exposure settings are not multiplied by crop factor.** The same aperture, shutter speed, and ISO still produce the same exposure brightness under the same lighting, within normal camera metering differences.
- **Perspective is not directly changed by sensor size.** Perspective depends on camera position. If you move to restore the same framing, the perspective changes because you moved.

## The key distinction

Sensor size influences several things at once: field of view, total light captured, and the depth-of-field or background-blur results possible with a given setup. Keep those ideas separate before combining them under the word “equivalent.”
